## What Went Well

Amelia (Developer): "The dominant pattern was a single governed-mutation template that got reused and then hardened. Retention established it, sensitivity copied it, redaction tightened it, and 2.5 generalized the hardened version back across all three. Each story made the previous ones safer."

Winston (System Architect): "The architecture invariants from Epic 1 held under governance load. EventStore stayed the only write authority, contracts never leaked substrate or audit-sink internals, tenant access stayed fail-closed and ordered before any lookup, and projections stayed descriptive rather than authoritative."

Murat (Test Architect): "The audit-pairing safety net in Story 2.5 is the standout. It is a named, inventory-driven test that fails if any new audited aggregate command is added outside the explicit list. That converts a convention into an enforced contract."

John (Product Manager): "The epic kept compliance value visible. The output is reviewable governance: retention with rationale, attributed redaction, point-in-time evidence under current policy, governed audit records, and accountable privileged access."

Key strengths:

- Strong governed-mutation continuity: retention, sensitivity, and redaction share the same command/event/replay/audit/idempotency shape, and Story 2.5 centralized the fail-closed audit gate instead of leaving three divergent handlers.
- Audit pairing was treated as a hard success gate everywhere: no governance domain event is externally reported as successful unless durably correlated audit evidence exists for the same operation.
- Scope boundaries were documented directly in story records, especially around no source-event deletion, no durable export artifacts without an ADR, identifier-only segment/audit targets, and projection non-authority.
- Party-mode review and advanced-elicitation passes ran as separate pre-dev hardening for the early stories (2.1-2.3) and materially sharpened the audit, timestamp-authority, and disclosure language before implementation.
- Local tests grew monotonically with each story (460 → 486 → 513 → 533 → 556 → 572 → 595 → 625), and every governance failure mode got its own focused coverage.
- Readiness gates were used as binding input: the CORE status for `MarkSensitiveData`/`RedactMessageContent` and the `Current`-only projection-freshness default were applied directly in the stories that depend on them.

## Challenges And Friction

Amelia (Developer): "The hardest part was the audit-pairing ordering. It is easy to record audit evidence too early or accept mismatched evidence as proof. Redaction (2.4) got both wrong on the first pass, and 2.5 had to retrofit the fix into retention and sensitivity."

Winston (System Architect): "Several governance design points had to be re-clarified mid-epic: timestamp authority versus event-sequence authority, audit-record redaction versus message redaction, safe target identity for audit records, and how much projected governance state is allowed to influence anything."

Murat (Test Architect): "Two environment problems recurred. Story 2.4's full test run was blocked by sandbox-denied writes to generated coverage/output files, so its review fixes were only source-validated. And the build-process learnings record that Story 2.8 shipped a hardcoded `Now` time-dependent regression that was not caught until the Epic 5 Story 5.1 review."

Recurring challenges:

- Audit evidence pairing was the single most repeated review theme. Story 2.4 had two CRITICAL audit defects (audit-before-validation side effects, unverified returned evidence). Story 2.5 then had to backfill pre-audit validation and returned-evidence policy/timestamp matching into retention and sensitivity. Story 2.8 had a related HIGH defect where precondition audit was recorded `Succeeded` but the final delegate outcome was not separately audited.
- Target-key correctness recurred across stories. Story 2.3 found `SensitivityTargetKey` duplicated in three places and centralized it on `GovernanceTarget.ToTargetKey()`; Story 2.7 then found `GovernedTargetKind.AuditRecord` falling through to a non-unique `audit:` key when the handle was missing.
- Diagnostic non-disclosure kept leaking through non-JSON surfaces. `ToString()` overrides had to be added or fixed in 2.1 (rationale/policy), 2.7 (caller-echoed audit handle), and were a standing requirement in every governed contract.
- Authorization-principal and freshness correctness surfaced late. Story 2.8 used the correlation ID as the caller principal until review; it also returned review details under stale/rebuilding/unavailable freshness until fixed to fail closed.
- Environment/validation friction: Story 2.4 could not run the full suite under the sandbox, and the codex worker was unreliable enough on this host (per the build-process learnings) that source-of-truth polling and agent swaps were needed.

## Key Lessons

1. Audit pairing must validate evidence, not just request it.

   The Epic 2 failure pattern was treating an audit call as proof. The hardened rule, established by Story 2.4 review and generalized by Story 2.5, is: validate state/target/no-op before calling audit, then verify the returned evidence's policy reference and timestamp match the command, and only then dispatch the mutation. A success status alone is not pairing.

2. Centralize the safety invariant, then assert it with a named test.

   Story 2.5's `ConversationGovernanceAuditGate` and `GovernanceAuditPairingSafetyNetTest` show the pattern: one fail-closed boundary plus an inventory test that fails if a new audited mutation is added outside the list. This is what kept the three early handlers from drifting and what let Story 2.8 add privileged justification to the inventory cleanly.

3. Deduplicate derived-key logic at its source the first time.

   Target-key drift appeared twice (2.3 sensitivity, 2.7 audit-record). The correct home is `GovernanceTarget.ToTargetKey()`, and every reader and writer must call it. Hand-written switch duplication silently desyncs replay state from projection state.

4. Temporal and historical reads must honor current disclosure policy, not the anchor's era.

   Story 2.6 reinforced that reconstructing state "as of" a past anchor is not permission to reveal pre-redaction content. Current redaction state must suppress historical and later-materialized text, out-of-coverage anchors must fail closed, and incomplete sources must never return authoritative details.

5. Non-disclosure and fail-closed behavior extend to principals, freshness, and diagnostics.

   Story 2.8 showed that the correct caller principal must be a trusted parameter (not a correlation ID), that privileged decisions block on anything other than `Current` freshness by default, and that `ToString()` and query diagnostics must never echo caller input or raw handles. Safe JSON payloads are necessary but not sufficient.

- Preserve the "trust posture before timeline content" ordering; Epic 3 must render tenant scope, identity, temporal cursor, trust, completeness, freshness, and citation status from Conversations projections or command-availability metadata only.

- Keep client-side reconstruction out of scope; the UI must render server-owned trust states, which is why Epic 2 deliberately returned non-inferential fields.
- The redaction UI/accessibility/clipboard verification surfaces (the unbuilt 2.4.2-style scope) intersect Epic 3 read workflows; confirm where that disclosure-safety verification is owned before the investigation workspace renders redacted evidence.

Significant discovery assessment:

- No Epic 2 finding invalidates the overall Epic 3 direction.
- Epic 3 must account for the unbuilt redaction split-story surfaces, the `Current`-only freshness gate on trust-bearing reads, and the requirement that historical/temporal views keep honoring current disclosure policy.
- No PRD or Epic 3 rewrite is required from this retrospective alone.

## Team Agreements

- Treat audit pairing as a validated success gate: pre-audit state validation, returned-evidence policy/timestamp matching, and dispatch only after proven pairing.
- Centralize any new safety invariant in one boundary and assert it with a named inventory test before relying on it across handlers.
- Resolve derived-key logic (target keys) at its single source and call it from every reader and writer; never hand-duplicate the switch.
- Keep historical and temporal reads governed by current disclosure policy; an anchor's era is never permission to reveal pre-redaction content.
- Keep redacted/unavailable/withheld as explicit server-owned states; absence must not imply authorization, freshness, or safety.
- Do not start new durable governance surfaces (export, indexes, deletion, legal-hold automation) without checking the readiness gates and ADR triggers first.
- Treat "local story suite passed" as necessary but not sufficient; time-sensitive and cross-story behavior needs deterministic and release-gate evidence.
